1. Welcome: what is Business Continuity Management?

Business Continuity Management (BCM) is the discipline of making sure your organisation can keep delivering its most important products and services when something goes wrong — a system outage, a flood, a cyber attack, the loss of a key supplier or a key person. Instead of improvising during a disruption, a BCM programme prepares answers in advance: what matters most, what could stop it, how quickly it must come back, who does what, and how do we know our plans actually work?

Continuity360 walks you through that programme as a repeating cycle:

3Analyse business impactDecide which processes are critical and how quickly each must recover.Business Impact Analysis

10Prove itTrack compliance controls and produce professional evidence reports.Compliance & Reports
The example used throughout this guide. Every screenshot shows GeeksOnTap, a fictional managed-IT-services company with five departments (Finance, IT, HR, Compliance, Operations), six employees, eight risks, approved recovery and continuity plans, call trees, exercises, incidents and a crisis record. You can follow along by recreating the same records for your own organisation — the order of this guide is the order in which to enter them.

Step 1. Open your Continuity360 address in a browser (for example https://yourcompany.continuity360.example) and sign in with the email address and password your administrator gave you.
Step 2. After signing in you land on the Dashboard (also called Management Overview), your home page.

3. Step 1 — Set up your organisation

Everything else links back to these records, so enter them first, in this order.

3.1 Company

Step 1. Open Organisation → Companies and select Add. Enter the Company name (this name brands every PDF report), industry, country and website, then Save.

3.2 Departments

Step 2. Open Organisation → Departments and create one record per department (Finance, IT, HR…). Each needs the company, a department name and a responsible person (its continuity contact).

3.3 Locations, employees and vendors

Step 3. Add Locations (offices, data centres), then Employees — name, department, email, phone. Employees link people to departments so the Department View and reports can find them.
Step 4. Add Vendors your business depends on (payroll bureau, hosting provider) with the service they provide and contact details. Vendors reappear later as risk sources and application suppliers.
All linked records must belong to the same company — Continuity360 blocks cross-company links automatically. In a multi-tenant installation each customer has an entirely separate site and database.

4. Step 2 — Build the risk register

A risk is anything that could disrupt your business. The register lists them, scores them, and tracks what you are doing about each one.

The risk register list: each row shows score, rating and status at a glance.

4.1 Recording a risk

Step 1. Open Risk Management → Risk Register → Add. Give the risk a clear title (“Ransomware attack on managed infrastructure”), pick the category (Operational, Technology, People, Supplier, Environmental or Regulatory) and the responsible person (risk owner).
Step 2. Optionally link the business process the risk threatens — this link powers department filtering and recovery intelligence later.
Step 3. Score the risk using the 5×5 risk matrix (next section) or the two coloured 1–5 scales, describe it, and Save.

4.2 The 5×5 risk matrix and coloured scales

The clickable risk matrix: one click sets both likelihood and impact. Green cells are low risk, red cells critical.

Click any cell of the matrix: the row sets likelihood (1 = rare … 5 = almost certain) and the column sets impact (1 = negligible … 5 = severe). The cell colour previews the resulting rating. You can also click the two separate 1–5 scales, or simply type numbers — all three methods set the same fields. The score is calculated on the server as likelihood × impact:

ScoreRatingSuggested response
1–5LowAccept and monitor.
6–11MediumTreat when practical; review each cycle.
12–19HighTreat with a dated action plan.
20–25CriticalTreat urgently; escalate to management.
The risk status lifecycle is Open → Treating → Accepted / Closed. Accepting or closing a risk requires a written justification — the form will not save without one. This is your audit trail.

4.3 Residual assessments and treatments

Step 4. After controls exist, record a Risk Assessment: pick the risk, score the residual likelihood and impact (what is left after controls) and write the rationale.
Step 5. For risks you are actively reducing, add a Risk Treatment: title, strategy (Avoid / Reduce / Transfer / Accept), owner, start and due dates and the actions. Completing a treatment requires completion evidence, and the due date can never precede the start date.

5. Step 3 — Business Impact Analysis (BIA)

The BIA answers the most important question in BCM: what must come back first, and how fast?

5.1 Recovery targets, in plain language

TermStands forPlain meaning
RTORecovery Time ObjectiveHow quickly the process must be running again after a disruption. “Helpdesk back within 1 hour.”
RPORecovery Point ObjectiveHow much data you can afford to lose, measured backwards in time. “At most 15 minutes of tickets.”
MTPDMaximum Tolerable Period of DisruptionThe absolute limit before damage becomes unacceptable. Always longer than the RTO — the gap is time for the business to recover after IT does.

5.2 Creating business processes

The business process form: coloured duration pills set RTO/RPO/MTPD with one click — short targets green, long targets red.

Step 1. Open Business Impact Analysis → Business Processes → Add. Name the process, pick its department and owner, and set the criticality (Low–Critical).
Step 2. Click the coloured duration pills to set RTO, RPO and MTPD (or type minutes directly). Continuity360 enforces RTO < MTPD and explains each field under its label.
Step 3. Describe the process and its manual workaround — what the team does by hand while systems are down.

5.3 Impact assessments

An impact assessment scores four damage dimensions with the same coloured 1–5 scales.

Step 4. For each important process create an Impact Assessment: pick the process and date, then score the damage a disruption would cause on four dimensions — Financial, Operational, Reputational and Regulatory (1–5 each, clickable scales). The overall impact is set automatically to the highest of the four. Describe the disruption scenario and how the impact grows over time.
Impact assessments justify your recovery targets to auditors: a process with regulatory impact 5 and a 2-day RTO will raise questions — the BIA is where those numbers get honest.

6. Step 4 — Disaster recovery plans

A recovery plan is a technical runbook: the exact steps to restore one business process after a disruption.

An approved recovery plan: targets, contacts, ordered steps with owners and durations, and the full approval history.

6.1 Before the plan: recovery strategies

Step 1. Create a Recovery Strategy first (Disaster Recovery → Recovery Strategies): the general approach, such as “fail over to the cloud standby region”. Plans reference a strategy.

6.2 Writing the plan

Step 2. Open Disaster Recovery → Recovery Plans → Add. Link the business process and strategy, write the purpose/scope and the activation criteria (when exactly this plan kicks in).
Step 3. Set the plan’s RTO and RPO. They may not be looser than the business process’s own targets — a plan that recovers slower than the business tolerates is rejected on save.
Step 4. Add contacts (the recovery team with emails and phones) and the ordered recovery steps: instruction, responsible person, expected minutes and validation criteria. The summed step durations must fit inside the RTO.

6.3 The approval workflow

Recovery plans are controlled documents: Draft → In Review → Approved → Retired.

Step 5. The author selects Request Review. A different BCM Administrator reviews and selects Approve — the system refuses self-approval and requires a current review date. Approval stamps who approved and when, and the plan becomes a submitted, read-only document.
Any later change requires retiring the plan and creating an amendment, and every content change demands a written revision reason. Nothing in an approved plan changes silently.

7. Step 5 — Business continuity plans (BCP)

While a recovery plan restores one technical service, the BCP keeps the whole business running: who does what, where people work, how you communicate, and which recovery plans support it.

The corporate BCP: version, named reviewers, covered business units and linked building blocks.

7.1 Building blocks first

Step 1. Under Business Continuity Planning create your reusable pieces: Continuity Strategies (work from home, alternative site, manual processing…), Emergency Contacts, Recovery Teams (members from the contacts) and Continuity Procedures (ordered instructions for one scenario).

7.2 Writing the plan

Step 2. Create the Business Continuity Plan: title, purpose and scope, activation criteria, continuity and communication procedures, the business units covered (departments), and link your strategies, teams, procedures and related recovery plans.
Step 3. Name the three reviewers: manager reviewer, BCM reviewer and executive approver. Together with the author these must be four different, enabled people holding the matching roles.

7.3 Four-stage approval and what it protects

StageWho actsWhat happens
DraftAuthorFree editing; only the author may change or submit the draft.
Manager ReviewManager reviewerContent is frozen; any change forces a return to Draft.
BCM ReviewBCM AdministratorMethodology check against your programme standards.
Executive ApprovalExecutive approverFinal sign-off. Review date must be within one year. Approval captures a permission-filtered snapshot of the plan and everything it links.

7.4 Versioning, distribution and acknowledgement

Plans carry server-assigned versions: new plans start at 1.0; amendments are Minor (1.0 → 1.1) or Major (1.1 → 2.0), chosen before the first save of the amendment and immutable afterwards. Retire the old plan before amending; the replacement walks the full approval path again.

Step 4. After approval, distribute the plan: the owner or a BCM Administrator selects recipients identified by the plan itself. Each recipient opens the approval-time snapshot through a sign-in-protected page; Continuity360 records first open, open count and their explicit acknowledgement.
Step 5. Approved and retired snapshots can be downloaded as PDF or Word, labelled with version, approval date, requester and an “uncontrolled copy” notice.

The BCP Dashboard: plan counts, reviews due, plans under approval, and live testing/corrective-action measures.

Automatic review reminders notify plan owners 30, 7 and 1 day before the review date and weekly when overdue — in-app and by email where configured.

8. Step 6 — Call trees

A call tree is your emergency notification cascade: who starts it, and who phones whom, so that everyone learns about a disruption in minutes.

A call tree with its live cascade preview: the initiator at the top, indented branches below, phone numbers beside each name.

Step 1. Open Business Continuity Planning → Call Trees → Add. Name the tree, write when to activate it and the caller instructions (what each caller must say and record). Optionally link the BCP and/or department it belongs to.
Step 2. Add cascade members from your emergency contacts. Leave Called by empty for the initiator; for everyone else pick the member who phones them. Give each a cascade role. The live preview redraws the tree as you type and flags anyone unreachable.
Step 3. Set the status to Active when the cascade is complete. Activation is enforced: at least two members, an initiator, no cycles, no orphans, nobody phoning themselves, each contact only once.
Span of control: if anyone phones more than seven people, Continuity360 warns you (on save, in the preview and in printed reports) and suggests a department-level tree. Good practice is three to seven calls per caller. The recommended pattern is one corporate tree whose department leads cascade into their own department trees — exactly how the GeeksOnTap demo is built.
Printing: only Active (completed) call trees print in the BCP report and combined report — with the full hierarchy, roles, phones, emails and who each person calls. Draft trees never leak into documents.

9. Step 7 — Testing and exercises

An untested plan is a hope, not a plan. The Testing module records drills and exercises and tracks the fixes they expose.

A completed DR drill: scenario, objectives with assessed outcomes, participants, result and lessons learned.

9.1 Exercise types

TypeWhat it is
Tabletop ExerciseA facilitated discussion walking the team through a scenario on paper.
WalkthroughStepping through one plan or procedure end to end.
SimulationA realistic rehearsal without touching production systems.
DR DrillActually executing a recovery runbook against standby systems.
Full Interruption TestDeliberately failing over live operations — the strongest, riskiest test.

9.2 Running an exercise

Step 1. Create the exercise: name, type, facilitator, the BCP and/or recovery plan being tested, scheduled date and the scenario (clearly simulated — no live systems are affected).
Step 2. Add objectives (“restore within the 240-minute RTO”) and participants with their exercise roles.
Step 3. Afterwards, assess every objective (Met / Partially Met / Not Met), record the conducted date, the overall result (Effective … Ineffective), the achieved recovery time, a results summary and lessons learned, then set status to Completed. The form refuses completion until all of that evidence exists.

9.3 Corrective actions

Step 4. For every gap found, raise a Corrective Action: what must change, priority, owner and due date. Completing one requires written completion evidence and stamps the completion date. Open corrective actions appear on the BCP Dashboard until closed.

10. Step 8 — Incident management

When a disruption actually happens, record it as an incident — the factual log of what happened, who responded and how it was resolved.

A resolved Critical incident: severity, affected process, activated plans, escalation chain and the full timeline.

Step 1. Open Incident & Crisis → Incidents → Add. Record the title, type (IT Outage, Cyber Security, Facility, Supplier, People, Data Loss, Other), severity (Low–Critical), who reported it, the incident manager, detection time and what happened.
Step 2. Link the affected business process, the related risk (reality checking your register), and the recovery plan / BCP / call tree you activated.
Step 3. Define the escalation chain: level 1, 2, 3… with a named person and the criteria (“if not mitigated within 30 minutes”). Levels must be unique. Creating a High or Critical incident automatically alerts the incident manager and everyone on the chain.
Step 4. Keep the timeline as you work: detection, escalations, actions, communications. Every status change (Open → Investigating → Mitigated → Resolved → Closed) is appended to the timeline automatically with who changed it.
Resolving or closing requires a resolution time after the detection time and a written resolution summary. Capture the root cause and impact summary too — the incident report prints all of it.

11. Step 9 — Crisis management

Some incidents outgrow normal handling. A crisis event adds command structure: one commander, clear decisions, controlled communication.

A closed Level 2 crisis: commander and deputy, triggering incident, activated plans, decision log and communications issued.

Step 1. Declare the crisis: title, level (1 Site / 2 Significant / 3 Organisation-wide), the crisis commander and an optional deputy (must be a different person), the triggering incident, the call tree activated and the situation summary.
Step 2. Record the activated BCPs and recovery plans, and keep the decision and action log — every decision, action and situation update with its time. Status changes log themselves.
Step 3. Track communications issued: audience (Employees, Customers, Regulators, Suppliers, Media, Executives), channel, who is responsible, the message summary and when it was sent.
Step 4. Stand down when stable: a stand-down time after the declaration and an outcome summary are mandatory before the status can move to Stood Down or Closed.
Communication Plans (same menu) are pre-approved templates per audience and channel — trigger, message template, owner and approver — so nobody has to draft a customer notice at 2 a.m. They print in the Incident and Crisis report.

12. Step 10 — Infrastructure and Recovery Intelligence

This module connects your technology to your business: which applications support which processes, what they run on, and — the differentiator — what an outage of any component would mean.

12.1 Building the inventory

The application inventory: criticality, hosting model and supported processes for each application.

Step 1. Under Infrastructure add your Applications (criticality, hosting model AWS/Azure/SaaS/on-premises, vendor, recovery plan and the business processes each supports), Servers (environment, platform, OS) and Databases (engine, platform, hosting server).
Step 2. Record Dependencies: application X Runs On server Y, Stores Data In database Z, or Integrates With application W. Each dependency names exactly one target, and an application can never depend on itself. These links are the nervous system of recovery intelligence.

12.2 Simulating an outage

The Recovery Intelligence walkthrough after simulating a database outage: six steps from detection to the saved post-incident record.

Step 3. Open Infrastructure → Recovery Intelligence, pick a component type (Application, Server or Database) and a specific component, then select Simulate outage.

Continuity360 walks the classic six steps using your data:

StepWhat it shows
1. Incident detectedThe simulated failed component, clearly labelled as an illustrative workflow.
2. Business impact analysisEvery affected application — including downstream ones found through the dependency graph — plus the business processes, their RTOs and the departments hit.
3. Recovery plan activatedThe BCPs covering those departments and the Active call trees to ring.
4. Recovery procedureThe approved recovery runbooks with their RTO/RPO targets.
5. Recovery validationGuidance to record restoration and data validation as an incident.
6. Post-incident reportThe simulation is saved as an Outage Simulation record with the whole walkthrough, and appears in the Infrastructure report.
Simulations reason over the relationships you recorded — there is no live connection to your cloud accounts. They are perfect for tabletop exercises: “the helpdesk database just died — walk me through it.”

13. Step 11 — Compliance

The compliance control register: framework references, self-assessed status, owners and evidence.

Step 1. Open Compliance → Control Register → Add. Enter the framework (for example “ISO 22301:2019”), the clause reference, a control title, the owner, your self-assessed status (Not Assessed / In Progress / Implemented / Not Applicable), the evidence reference and the next review date.
ISO 22301 and climate change. Amendment 1:2024 added two clause-4 requirements: determine whether climate change is a relevant BCMS issue (4.1) and note that interested parties may have climate requirements (4.2). Cover it with an Environmental-category risk for your climate threats plus one compliance control documenting your determination — the GeeksOnTap demo includes a worked example.
Continuity360 ships no standards content — you enter your own framework references and self-assessments. Reports label compliance data accordingly and nothing claims certification on your behalf.

14.1 The Department View: everything about one department

Department View for Finance: processes, assessments, risks, treatments, plans and people in one page.

Step 1. Open Organisation → Department View, pick a department from the dropdown, and get its complete continuity picture: processes with targets, impact assessments, risks and residual assessments, treatments, BCPs covering it, recovery plans and employees — each with counts and links.

14.2 The Report Centre

The Report Centre: one department selector scopes every report; the Complete BCM Report combines all areas.

14.3 Document control, built in

Every generated report carries a cover page (your company name, title, scope, an auto-incrementing document version per report and department, prepared-by with full name, and a document history of recent issues), a table of contents, colour-coded badges throughout, the department and report name on every page header, and “Page N of M” plus the version in every footer. BCP and DR reports include a Document control and approvals section naming who prepared, reviewed and approved each plan — with real names. Reports include only records the requesting user may see and are labelled uncontrolled copies.

15.1 Subscription plans and what they unlock

PlanIncluded modules

Your current plan shows in the sidebar footer. Modules outside your plan disappear from navigation, and creating records in them is blocked server-side with a clear upgrade message. Plans are set centrally by the Continuity360 operator; tenants can see but never change their own plan.

15.2 White labelling (Enterprise)

Enterprise tenants may set a display name, a primary colour (six-digit hex) and a report footer line in the C360 Branding settings. The name and colour theme the navigation and every PDF report; clearing the fields instantly restores the default Continuity360 identity.

15.3 API integrations (Enterprise)

Register Integration Connections for AWS, Azure, Microsoft 365 or monitoring platforms: a connection name, the account/tenant reference (never a secret) and an encrypted credential slot. The inventory import accepts a normalised JSON list (for example produced from aws ec2 describe-instances) and creates or updates your applications, servers and databases automatically, stamping the connection’s last import time. Live credential-based polling is on the roadmap and clearly labelled.
